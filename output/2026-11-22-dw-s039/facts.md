# Antikythera Mechanism: ancient bronze gear computer

## What it is
- A bronze geared device about 2,000 years old, found in a shipwreck off Antikythera (small Greek island).
- Hand-cranked "analogue computer" that showed sun and moon positions and predicted eclipses.
- Now in the National Archaeological Museum, Athens.

## How it works / how it happened
- Wreck sank around 70-60 BC (script); a wooden box with the device sank with the ship.
- 1900: sponge divers from Symi island found the wreck.
- 1901: items recovered from about 45 m depth; a stone-like lump was ignored for about two years in Athens museum.
- May 17, 1902: Valerios Stais noticed a gear inside the lump.
- Gears work like bicycle gears: different tooth counts change the rotation ratio; a handle turns the train.
- Front: zodiac ring (12 signs) and an Egyptian-style calendar ring; a ball showed moon phase.
- Moon pointer speeds up and slows, using a pin in a slotted gear.
- Back dials: 19-year calendar cycle (235 months); Saros dial (223 months, about 18 years) with 38 lunar and 27 solar eclipse marks; four-year dial for Greek games.
- Research: Derek de Solla Price started in 1951; 2005 X-ray CT scans revealed hidden inscriptions, including manual-like text.
- 2021: UCL Tony Freeth team built a model with sun, moon and five planets (planet gears not found; still a proposal).
- 2024: Glasgow University statistics found about 354 holes in the calendar ring (lunar year), not 365; Freeth disagrees.
- 2025: preprint says turning the handle about four months ahead could jam the gears; not checked by other experts.

## Why
- The device is based on Greek astronomy theory, so any error in that theory shows up in its output.
- Its tooth spacing (about 1.6 mm) was cut by hand, showing high craftsmanship.

## Twist / surprising facts
- Possible makers: Rhodes (Hipparchus or Posidonius), Syracuse (Archimedes' school) or Corinth colonies (month names) - no proof.
- Date guesses: 205 BC to 87 BC.
- Similar gear complexity reappears only in 14th-century European astronomical clocks, about 1,400 years later.
- Largest gear about 13 cm with 223 teeth.
- 82 fragments found; at least 30 bronze gears remain.
- Handle never found; box size about a shoe box (estimate).
