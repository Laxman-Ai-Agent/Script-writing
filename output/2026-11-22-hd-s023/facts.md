# Constipation

## Entry/trigger
- Waste entering the colon loses water; slow transit makes stool dry and hard.
- Low dietary fibre (indigestible plant material that holds water) leaves stool hard.
- Low water intake, prolonged sitting, suppressing toilet urges, stress and routine changes during travel are contributors.
- Drugs: opioid painkillers, iron tablets, some antacids, some blood pressure and depression medicines.
- Conditions: low thyroid, diabetes, IBS; also pregnancy, post-delivery and old age.
- Women are affected about twice as often as men.

## Cellular chain
- Food is digested and absorbed in the small intestine; the residue moves into the colon.
- Colon wall muscles move waste forward in waves.
- The colon absorbs water; longer transit leads to dry, hard stool.
- The rectum stores stool; when full, a signal reaches the brain and creates the urge.
- Pelvic floor muscles must relax during defecation; poor coordination of these muscles can impair evacuation.

## Immune/organ impact
- Hard stool stuck in the rectum (faecal impaction) can let liquid stool leak around it, often mistaken for diarrhoea.
- Prolonged straining can enlarge veins near the anus (piles).
- Straining can cause small tears at the anus (fissure).
- Rarely, the rectum can slip out (prolapse).

## Symptom science
- Straining and pain come from hard stool passing through.
- Bloating, abdominal pain and nausea: listed without a mechanism.
- Confusion or behaviour change in older adults, especially with dementia: listed as a sign.

## Numbers & citations
- About 1 in 8 people worldwide have constipation; about 1 in 5 consult a doctor (unnamed).
- NIH: adults should consume 22-34 g of fibre per day.
