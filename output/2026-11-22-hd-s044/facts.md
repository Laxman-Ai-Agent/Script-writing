# Pneumonia

## Entry/trigger
- Germs in droplets from coughs and sneezes are inhaled and travel down to the air sacs.
- Pathogens: bacteria (Streptococcus pneumoniae is the main bacterial cause in children, per WHO); viruses (RSV, flu, COVID-19); fungi (rare, mostly in people with weak immunity).
- Food or liquid entering the lungs by accident can also cause it.
- Often follows a flu-like infection.
- Higher risk: very young children, people over 65, smokers, heavy drinkers, malnourished people, and people with diabetes, heart failure, or lung, kidney or liver disease.
- Household smoke from wood or dung cooking, indoor smoking and crowded housing increase risk in children (WHO).

## Cellular chain
- Alveoli are the small air sacs where oxygen passes into the blood.
- Germs reach the alveoli; the immune system responds with inflammation.
- Alveoli fill with fluid or pus instead of air.
- Less oxygen passes into the blood; the script links cough, breathlessness and fever to this process.

## Immune/organ impact
- Bacteria can enter the bloodstream (bacteraemia), which can progress to septic shock.
- Fluid can collect around the lungs; a pus pocket inside the lung is a lung abscess.
- Oxygen transfer can fall enough to cause respiratory failure.
- Kidney damage in some cases.

## Symptom science
- Cough (with or without phlegm): NHS says cough helps expel the infection from the lungs.
- Chest pain on breathing or coughing: from lung inflammation.
- Breathlessness: from fluid-filled air sacs.
- Older adults may show low temperature or confusion instead of fever.
- Infants may grunt while breathing.

## Numbers & citations
- WHO (2019): pneumonia caused 14% of deaths in children under 5, about 740,000 children.
- WHO: only about 1 in 3 children who need antibiotics receive them.
- CDC: pneumonia can occur in any season.
